\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

Label provenance defines the achievable performance ceiling for peptide-MHC binding prediction; architecture choice is secondary. Across three labelling strategies, label source accounted for $\sim$29\,pp of accuracy variation, dwarfing the $\sim$11\,pp maximum inter-architecture gap, and the variation concentrated exclusively in the WB class (F1 0.723--0.925). This concentration is not incidental: the 50--500\,nM affinity range defining the WB boundary is precisely where experimental binding probabilities are most graded, where ML predictors trained on finite data produce the most uncertain labels, and where conformational flexibility renders sequence-only approximations least reliable.

The three-tier label experiment --- PSSM (94.8\%) $\rightarrow$ MHCflurry (91.9\%) $\rightarrow$ random (65.8\%) --- provides direct quantitative evidence for a label contamination cascade centred on the WB class. Deterministic biophysical rules (PSSM) yield the cleanest decision boundary because the model learns a ground-truth additive function. ML-derived labels (MHCflurry), themselves trained on IEDB data that is $>$55\% computationally labelled \citep{Preibisch26}, already encode non-linear inter-position dependencies that reduce apparent performance; the 2.9\,pp gap represents information loss from learning to approximate an approximation. If such predictions are re-deposited into public databases, each successive predictor generation compounds these biases. We propose that the WB F1 --- a metric that collapses to zero when a model ignores the class --- be reported as a standard diagnostic: a declining WB F1 across predictor generations would function as an early-warning signal for accelerating label contamination.

BLOSUM62 proved more robust than ESM-2 at single-allele training scales; high-dimensional per-position embeddings overfit small datasets. Architectural complexity beyond depth with batch normalisation gave diminishing returns, and a simple PSSM baseline (94.8\%) exceeded all neural networks --- underscoring that biophysical knowledge should be a standard comparator in computational benchmarking.

The multi-allele extension shows the recipe is portable: one rebalanced pan model replaces a per-allele panel across five alleles and reaches 0.819 pooled sensitivity on 15 alleles, approximating NetMHCpan's practical value with a simple DFFN. Yet non-canonical (motif-failing) binders plateau at $\sim$0.65 sensitivity across sequence- and structure-based predictors alike; the energy-compensated tail (P2-Lys/Arg/His reaching B-pocket Glu63) is prediction-resistant to all current methods. The three-tier screening pipeline we considered (sequence filtering $\rightarrow$ structural scoring $\rightarrow$ energy-minimisation validation) therefore remains a hypothesis for these configurations, pending experimental validation --- consistent with the finding that the canonical binder problem is effectively solved while the non-canonical tail defines the current frontier.

\subsection{Limitations}
Training labels are MHCflurry-derived rather than experimental affinities; we quantified the circularity by cross-referencing the 49 IEDB epitopes against MHCflurry's training corpus --- 44/49 (89.8\%) overlapped, though all five non-overlapping epitopes were correctly classified. The single-allele design was deliberate --- holding the allele constant isolates label-source variation --- but the multi-allele extension addresses generalisability directly. The multi-allele estimates rest on IEDB-derived positives; experimental (non-IEDB) validation of the extreme tail is outstanding, and a pMHC-fine-tuned structure model might lift the Type B plateau. The K-E63 salt bridge, all translational predictions, and the cancer neoepitope candidates require experimental confirmation (e.g., IFN-$\gamma$ ELISpot or tetramer staining). Antigen-processing steps (proteasomal cleavage, TAP transport) are not modelled.
