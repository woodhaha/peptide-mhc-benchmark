## Abstract

**Motivation**: Peptide-MHC-I binding-prediction benchmarks can manufacture false conclusions when confounders are uncontrolled. **Results**: We benchmarked six architectures on 5,190 MHCflurry-labelled HLA-A\*02:01 9-mers with multi-seed estimates, and re-analysed three apparent findings under controlled conditions. A convolutional network was modestly best (91.9% ± 0.9% vs 90.6% ± 1.2% for a deep feed-forward network); random labels fell to chance (~33%); and a claimed 'label quality dominates architecture' result dissolved once the degenerate null control was removed. A zero-parameter anchor rule matched the trained model on canonical IEDB peptides, while non-canonical binders plateaued at ~0.65 sensitivity across three independent sequence predictors (15-allele panel) — the true frontier. **Availability**: Code, models, and data at github.com/woodhaha/peptide-mhc-benchmark. **Contact**: zhouzhuha@wmu.edu.cn.

## 1 Introduction

MHC class I molecules present 8–11-mer peptides to CD8+ T cells, and accurate prediction of peptide–MHC-I binding underpins epitope discovery for vaccine design and cancer immunotherapy (Falk et al., 1991; Lundegaard et al., 2011). For HLA-A\*02:01 — the most prevalent class I allele globally — binding is dominated by two primary anchor positions: position 2 (p2, hydrophobic: Leu/Met/Ile/Val) and position 9 (p9, C-terminal, Val/Leu preferred) (Ruppert et al., 1993; Rammensee et al., 1995). Computational predictors have evolved from position-specific scoring matrices to neural networks and pan-specific tools such as NetMHCpan (Reynisson et al., 2020), MHCflurry (O'Donnell et al., 2020), BigMHC (Albert et al., 2023), and MUNIS (Wohlwend et al., 2025), increasingly using protein language model embeddings (Rives et al., 2021).

Several gaps remain. Head-to-head architecture comparisons under fully controlled conditions (identical training data, encoding, evaluation) are uncommon. The impact of training-label quality on downstream performance is seldom quantified, despite evidence that >55% of IEDB entries are computationally rather than experimentally labelled (Preibisch et al., 2026). And the translation of binding prediction to real-world epitope discovery is rarely demonstrated in a unified, reproducible pipeline.

We address these gaps through a systematic benchmark designed to isolate the effects of architecture choice, label quality, and encoding. Our contributions are: (i) a controlled six-architecture comparison with multi-seed uncertainty estimates, showing a modest but measurable architecture effect; (ii) a label-source control confirming that random labels fall to chance; (iii) evaluation of ESM-2 embeddings against BLOSUM62 with a mean-pooled ablation; (iv) external validation against 49 IEDB epitopes; (v) translational application through protein scanning and cancer mutation analysis; (vi) a template-based structural hypothesis for non-canonical anchoring; and (vii) a multi-allele extension testing whether the recipe generalizes across common HLA-I alleles and how hard non-canonical binders are for three independent sequence-based predictors.

## 2 Methods

### 2.1 Training data and labels

100,000 random 9-mer peptides were generated by uniform sampling of the 20 standard amino acids. Each was labelled with MHCflurry 2.2.1 against HLA-A\*02:01 and assigned to Strong Binder (SB, percentile rank <0.5%), Weak Binder (WB, 0.5%≤rank<2.0%), or Non-Binder (NB, rank ≥2.0%) following the netMHCpan convention (Reynisson et al., 2020). The resulting distribution (1.7% SB, 5.1% WB, 93.2% NB) was balanced by downsampling to 1,730 per class (n=5,190), partitioned 90/10 into training (n=4,671) and held-out test (n=519) sets with stratified sampling (seed 42). Two comparator labelling strategies were implemented: a PSSM encoding crystallographic A\*02:01 anchor preferences, and random synthetic labels as a baseline control. An external benchmark of 49 experimentally validated IEDB epitopes plus 20 homopolymer negatives was curated.

### 2.2 Peptide encoding

Each 9-mer was encoded with the BLOSUM62 matrix (Henikoff and Henikoff, 1992), min–max normalized, producing a 9×20 matrix flattened to 180 dimensions for fully connected networks. ESM-2 embeddings (Lin et al., 2023) were extracted at t6 (2,880-dim per-peptide; 320 per position × 9) and t12 (4,320-dim; 480 per position × 9) depths — the two smallest ESM-2 checkpoints (8M and 35M parameters); a mean-pooled t6 variant (320-dim) served as an ablation control destroying positional information.

### 2.3 Model architectures

Six classifiers were implemented: (i) FFN (Nielsen et al., 2003): 180→90→3, 49K params; (ii) Deep FFN: 180→360→180→90→45→3 with batch normalisation and dropout, 152K params; (iii) CNN: Conv2D(32,3×3) + FFN body, 1.05M params; (iv) LSTM(64)→dense(32)→3, 24K params; (v) ResNet-style stem + 3 residual blocks, 325K params; (vi) Random Forest (100 trees). For ESM-2 embeddings an FFN classifier with a 256→128→64 stack was trained. All networks used cross-entropy loss with Adam or RMSprop (lr 0.001), 150 epochs, batch size 50, 20% internal validation split, early stopping (patience 10), and learning-rate reduction on plateau. Five-fold stratified cross-validation was performed for the FFN. The training/test split is specified; the held-out test set (519 peptides) was disjoint from training and used for all reported external metrics, for all reported metrics.

### 2.4 Protein scanning, mutation analysis, and structure

Ten therapeutically relevant proteins (MART-1, gp100, tyrosinase, NY-ESO-1, WT1, p53, KRAS, influenza M1, CMV pp65, SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD) were scanned with a sliding 9-mer window (stride 1; 3,536 windows). Sixteen recurrent hotspot mutations (7 p53, 9 KRAS) were analyzed by comparing wild-type and mutant binding scores; mutations were classified as Created/Enhanced/Destroyed/Unchanged. The KRAS G12V neoepitope YKLVVVGAV carries a non-canonical lysine at P2; we evaluated a predicted K-E63 salt bridge using comparative B-pocket geometry across eight HLA-A\*02:01 crystal structures (PDB 1DUZ, 1AKJ, 1HHJ, 1JF1, 1QEW, 1QRN, 1S9W, 2GIT), energy minimisation (OpenMM 8.5.2, amber14 force field), and a Coulombic electrostatic estimate. Longer molecular-dynamics trajectories (100 ns) were run as an exploratory companion analysis and are reported separately in the data archive; the structural argument here is deliberately restricted to minimisation and qualitative electrostatics.

### 2.5 Feature extraction

Learned features were extracted from the penultimate dense layer via truncated models. Hierarchical clustering (Ward's method, Euclidean distance) was applied to representations of top epitope candidates; feature–binding-score correlation was assessed by Pearson's r with a 1,000-iteration permutation test to control for chance correlations. t-SNE visualisation of the 45-dimensional penultimate layer was used to inspect class separation, and integrated gradients identified position-specific anchor contributions.

### 2.6 Multi-allele extension

Experimentally confirmed class I binders were curated from IEDB (Vita et al., 2019) for five common alleles (HLA-A\*02:01, A\*01:01, A\*03:01, B\*07:02, B\*44:03), yielding n=14,488 positives (canonical 11,225; Type A 151; Type B 3,112). Type A (tolerated weak anchors) and Type B (energy-compensated non-canonical) were defined by whether the peptide violates the allele's canonical anchor motif at p2/p9. Protein-background negatives were sampled from natural proteomes. A rebalanced pan-specific DFFN (B\*07:02 upsampled 4×) was trained on pooled data with five seeds (0, 1, 42, 123, 2024). MHCflurry 2.2.1 (affinity <500 nM) and MUNIS (Wohlwend et al., 2025; ESM-2-based sequence model) served as independent comparators on the n=3,112 Type B population plus canonical controls. A 15-allele panel (pan16: 11 additional alleles) tested scaling with per-allele minimum sample (2,000) upsampling. To address the label-circularity concern, temporal holdout compared IEDB epitopes published after 2022 (post-2022, unseen by MHCflurry's ~pre-2021 training) against pre-2021 (in-distribution) epitopes at sensitivity threshold 0.95. A broad-motif retraining control (extending the canonical anchor set to include tolerated residues) tested whether Type A under-recovery reflects missing training coverage.

## 3 Results

### 3.1 Training data and architecture comparison

MHCflurry labelling of 100,000 random 9-mers yielded 1.7% SB, 5.1% WB, 93.2% NB; after balancing, 5,190 peptides (1,730/class). Across five training seeds, the CNN achieved the highest accuracy (91.9% ± 0.9%; Table 1, Figure 1), modestly ahead of the Deep FFN (90.6% ± 1.2%), the FFN (89.8% ± 1.1%), and the LSTM (89.7% ± 0.9%) — the top four architectures within ~2 percentage points — while ResNet (87.6% ± 1.8%) and Random Forest (79.7% ± 0.4%) were clearly worse. MHCflurry itself scored 100% on the test set it labelled, quantifying label circularity (see Discussion). The WB class was consistently the most difficult (F1 0.752–0.880).

The modest spread among the top four architectures reflects the positional nature of peptide–MHC binding: anchor positions p2/p9 dominate, so architectures that preserve positional information perform comparably. ResNet and the Random Forest were clearly worse, consistent with the absence of translation invariance in a 9×20 encoding and, for the Random Forest, the high dimensionality of the BLOSUM62 encoding.

### 3.2 Label-source controls

Label source, not architecture, sets the practical ceiling of what a model can learn (Table 2, Figure 2). Training on MHCflurry labels reached 90.6% accuracy, whereas a deterministic PSSM label source reached 82.6% (on a separate 15,000-peptide pool, so not a clean head-to-head comparison) and an i.i.d. random-label null fell to chance (34.5 ± 1.7%, close to the 33.3% expected for three balanced classes). The random-label result confirms the models learn genuine signal rather than noise; the PSSM–MHCflurry gap, though confounded by pool size, suggests MHCflurry's labels are at least as learnable as a hand-crafted scoring matrix.

### 3.3 Encoding

BLOSUM62 generalized stably under cross-validation (92.2 ± 0.4%; per-fold 92.2/92.4/92.1/92.7/91.6%). ESM-2 t6 per-position embeddings scored 93.3% on a single split but collapsed to 82.2 ± 1.0% under CV — the 2,880-dimensional features overfit the 4,671-sample training set (Table 3, Figure 7). Mean-pooled t6 embeddings collapsed to 65.9%, confirming that positional information is essential. The t12 variant (4,320-dim) overfit similarly (90.9–91.5%). The single-split 93.3% result reflects a favourable partition rather than a genuine encoding advantage at this training scale; at the data scale typical of single-allele studies, compact biologically motivated encodings such as BLOSUM62 remain the more robust choice, and ESM-2's representational advantage may require substantially larger training corpora to materialise.

### 3.4 External validation and translation

The Deep FFN achieved 95.8% sensitivity (46/48), 75.0% specificity (15/20), ROC AUC 0.947 against IEDB epitopes (Table 5, Figure 3), after excluding `ILRGSVAHK` (influenza NP 265–273), which is HLA-B\*08:01- rather than A\*02:01-restricted. The two remaining false negatives (`QYDPVAALF`, `TLGIVCPIC`) carried non-canonical p9 anchors (F, C). An independent predictor, NetMHCpan-4.1, achieved 83.3% sensitivity (40/48) on the same set. This gap partly reflects label circularity (44/49 of the epitopes overlap MHCflurry's training corpus), but NetMHCpan's pan-specific training on MS-elution-based labels makes this an indicative rather than clean comparison; the more direct circularity evidence is the 62.24% label agreement reported in the Discussion. To contextualise the benchmark, we include a zero-parameter anchor-checker baseline (a rule classifying any 9-mer with canonical p2/p9 residues as a binder): it achieves sensitivity 1.0 and F1 0.85–1.0 on this canonical-motif-dominated set, matching the trained model (a comparison with only 3 discordant peptides, too low-powered for a formal test) — confirming that the canonical set overstates generalisation and that the informative signal lies in the non-canonical/hard-tail sensitivity. The five false positives were homopolymers (≤2 unique amino acids) with canonical anchors — biologically implausible binders whose low sequence complexity the classifier fails to penalise. A domain-knowledge filter (≤2 unique amino acids → non-binder) removed all five, but we report 75.0% as the primary, unfiltered specificity to avoid post-hoc inflation. Protein scanning of 10 proteins (3,536 windows) recovered 10/11 known epitopes (91%; Figure 4), including immunodominant `GILGFVFTL` (M1), `NLVPMVATV` (pp65), and `SLLMWITQC` (NY-ESO-1). Mutation scanning of 16 hotspot mutations identified 7 epitope-altering events (Table 4, Figure 5). p53 R248W created the neoepitope `MNWRPILTI` (R→W at p3 introduces a bulky hydrophobic residue improving groove fit); KRAS G12V created `YKLVVVGAV` via G→V at p9 creating a canonical C-terminal anchor. p53 R248W is among the most frequent TP53 missense mutations across cancers (~3.5% of somatic TP53 mutations, IARC); KRAS G12C enhanced an existing epitope (WB→SB), suggesting synergy with pharmacological G12C inhibition. Three mutations (KRAS G13D, KRAS A146T, p53 R249S) destroyed existing epitopes, representing potential immune-evasion mechanisms.

### 3.5 Structural hypothesis

Template-based analysis of eight HLA-A\*02:01 crystal structures (Figure 8) showed a conserved B-pocket with Glu63 positioned 4.7 ± 0.2 Å from the P2 Cα. Energy minimisation refined the P2 Cα–Glu63 OE2 distance to 3.7 Å, within the reach of a lysine side chain; note that a proper salt-bridge geometry requires the side-chain Nζ–OE2 distance rather than the backbone Cα — which we did not compute here, so the Cα-based distances are a coarse proxy only — and an un-solvated Coulombic estimate would overstate the stabilisation (a buried salt bridge typically contributes only a few kcal/mol after desolvation). We therefore present only qualitatively the hypothesis that a charged P2 lysine could achieve stable binding through electrostatic compensation, pending a solvated free-energy calculation and experimental validation (e.g., X-ray crystallography or cryo-EM of the YKLVVVGAV–HLA-A\*02:01 complex).

In contrast, the p53 R248W neoepitope `MNWRPILTI` carries asparagine at P2, and its improved predicted binding relative to wild-type (`MNRRPILTI`) is attributable to the P3 Arg→Trp substitution enhancing hydrophobic packing rather than any P2-mediated mechanism. The KRAS G12V enhanced peptide `LVVVGAVGV`, with valine at P2, represents the canonical hydrophobic anchor configuration and served as a positive control; its P2 Cα–Glu63 distance of 4.2 Å is consistent with optimal B-pocket accommodation.

### 3.6 Multi-allele generalization

With protein-background negatives, per-allele models reached non-canonical sensitivity 0.84–0.96, and a rebalanced pan model matched them on four of five alleles (A\*02:01 0.84, A\*01:01 0.88, A\*03:01 1.00, B\*44:03 1.00); B\*07:02 was a notable exception (pan 0.60 vs per-allele 0.96; 0.64 in the 15-allele pan16 panel, Table 6), consistent with over-anchoring to P2-Proline (Figure 9). This corrects an earlier observation that per-allele models outperform pan models on non-canonical binders: that apparent advantage was a 10.6× training-data-size artifact, not biology.

On the 15-allele panel, pooled non-canonical sensitivity was 0.819 (307/375), with ~11/15 alleles at 0.76–1.0 (Table 6). The weak tail (B\*27:05, B\*51:01, B\*07:02, A\*24:02) reflected data scarcity and, for B\*07:02, over-anchoring, not architecture. Temporal holdout (three alleles with sufficient post-2022 data) confirmed preserved recognition of epitopes published after the labeler's training cutoff at sens@0.95: A\*02:01 0.824, A\*03:01 0.936, B\*07:02 0.973 (versus pre-2021 0.879/0.905/0.898), indicating the model is not overfit to a single training-data era.

### 3.7 Non-canonical plateau

Canonical binders were recognized at ~0.98 by every predictor class: DFFN 0.979, MHCflurry 0.986, MUNIS 0.950. Non-canonical (Type B) binders plateaued at ~0.65: DFFN 0.607, MHCflurry 0.668, MUNIS 0.647 (Table 7) — a second, ESM-2-based sequence model did not lift the ceiling. Type A (tolerated weak anchors) binders were inherently hard (DFFN 0.477, MHCflurry 0.669) and not recovered by broad-motif retraining (A\*03:01 flat at 0.634; B\*07:02 Type B dropped 0.680→0.341). The extreme energy-compensated tail (e.g., KRAS G12V P2-Lys; P2-Lys/Arg/His sidechains (maximum extension 7.6/7.3/4.4 Å) span the 3.83 Å P2–Glu63 gap) was scored as non-binder by all three predictors. Note that Table 4's "CREATED" label for KRAS G12V refers to the motif-level creation of a canonical P9 anchor (G→V) relative to wild-type, not to a predicted binder: the mutant's absolute score remains below the binding threshold owing to the non-canonical P2-Lys.

### 3.8 Learned feature representations

Feature extraction from the penultimate dense layer revealed biologically meaningful structure (Figure 6). t-SNE visualisation of the 45-dimensional penultimate layer showed partial class separation: SB peptides clustered distinctly (correct classification rate 91%), while WB peptides formed a continuous transition zone between SB and NB clusters — consistent with the graded affinity boundary in the 50–500 nM range. Misclassified peptides (primarily WB–NB boundary cases) mapped to the cluster overlap region, confirming that representation quality degrades in the same ambiguity zone where label contamination has the greatest impact. Integrated-gradients analysis identified position-specific anchor contributions: p9 dominated SB predictions, p2 and p3 contributed most to WB predictions, and NB predictions were driven by the absence of anchor-characteristic patterns. Hierarchical clustering separated validated epitopes into coherent clusters, with false negatives forming a distinct branch characterised by absent anchor-position activation. Five features showed Pearson r > 0.7 with binding score; by a 1,000-iteration permutation test, approximately 0.3 features would exceed this threshold by chance, suggesting these represent genuine binding-relevant representations rather than spurious correlations.

## 4 Discussion

### Methodological warnings

Three apparent findings in this project dissolved under controlled re-analysis, each illustrating how benchmark composition can manufacture false conclusions. First, an apparent per-allele superiority over a pan model was a 10.6× training-data-size artifact, not biology. Second, a claimed 'label quality dominates architecture' result collapsed once the degenerate random-label null (which performed at chance, ~33%) was removed — the realistic label effect is smaller than the architecture range. Third, a reported 'simple architecture beats complex ones' ranking was an artifact of stale model checkpoints: re-training with fixed seeds showed a CNN instead modestly outperforms the Deep FFN. We recommend that peptide-MHC benchmarks report multi-seed estimates with confidence intervals, protein-background negatives, frozen data snapshots, and a state-of-the-art comparator such as NetMHCpan, to avoid these failure modes.

Architecture choice has a modest but measurable effect on peptide-MHC binding prediction. Across five seeds, a CNN (91.9%) outperformed a Deep FFN (90.6%) by ~1.3 percentage points, while a Random Forest was clearly worse (79.7%), for a maximum inter-architecture range of ~12 percentage points. The top four architectures fell within ~2 percentage points, indicating that data quality and the decision boundary matter more than architectural complexity for this task. Random-label controls performed at chance (~33%), confirming that the observed performance reflects genuine signal rather than noise overfitting.

A random-label control performed at chance (~33%), confirming the models do not overfit to noise, and a PSSM-derived label source yielded lower accuracy than MHCflurry labels (82.6% vs 90.6%, though on a separate peptide pool and therefore not a clean comparison). This does not support the hypothesis that deterministic biophysical labels are inherently superior to ML-derived labels; rather, it suggests that MHCflurry's labels are at least as learnable as a simple PSSM. We nevertheless emphasise that training on any computational label — including MHCflurry — re-deposits predictor bias into downstream models, and recommend that benchmarks report label provenance and the WB-class F1 as a standard diagnostic.

An independent state-of-the-art predictor, NetMHCpan-4.1, agreed with the MHCflurry labels on only 62.24% (323/519) of the same held-out test peptides — substantially below the 90.6% achieved by a model trained directly on those labels. This ~28-point gap quantifies the label-circularity limitation: our trained models learn MHCflurry's specific decision boundary, whereas an independent predictor disagrees on roughly one peptide in three, underscoring that the reported accuracies measure fidelity to a particular labeler rather than true binding.

BLOSUM62 proved more robust than ESM-2 at single-allele training scales; high-dimensional per-position embeddings overfit small datasets. The 2,880-dimensional per-position ESM-2 features create an unfavourable feature-to-sample ratio for the 4,671-sample training set, and the single-split 93.3% result reflects a favourable partition rather than a genuine encoding advantage. The shallower 6-layer ESM-2 variant outperformed the deeper 12-layer model, possibly because t12's additional transformer layers encode long-range protein-level interactions that introduce noise at the short 9-mer peptide scale. Architectural complexity beyond depth with batch normalisation gave diminishing returns; a simple PSSM label source yielded lower accuracy (82.6%) than MHCflurry labels on its own pool, showing that deep learning adds value over a hand-crafted scoring matrix for this task.

The multi-allele extension shows the recipe is portable: one rebalanced pan model matches per-allele specialists on four of five alleles (B\*07:02 excepted) and reaches 0.819 pooled sensitivity on 15 alleles, approximating NetMHCpan's practical value with a simple DFFN. Yet non-canonical (motif-failing) binders plateau at ~0.65 sensitivity across three independent sequence-based predictors: our Deep FFN (0.607), MHCflurry (0.668), and the ESM-2-based MUNIS (0.647) fall within a narrow band (0.61–0.67), far below the ~0.98 canonical sensitivity, indicating this ceiling is shared across sequence architectures of varying complexity rather than specific to our simple network. The energy-compensated tail (P2-Lys/Arg/His reaching B-pocket Glu63) is prediction-resistant to all three. The three-tier screening pipeline we considered (sequence filtering → structural scoring → energy-minimisation validation) remains a hypothesis for these configurations, pending experimental validation, since the structural analysis presented here (§3.5) is a single-case docking hypothesis rather than a scaled benchmark. The canonical binder problem is effectively solved; the non-canonical tail defines the current frontier.

### Translational implications

The cancer neoepitope candidates identified here are of translational interest as testable hypotheses. p53 R248W, which created `MNWRPILTI`, is among the most frequent TP53 missense mutations across human cancers (~3.5% of somatic TP53 mutations, IARC) with documented gain-of-function oncogenic activity; it is among the most frequent TP53 missense mutations across multiple tumour types (IARC TP53 database). A vaccine targeting this neoepitope could, only if immunogenicity is experimentally confirmed, apply across multiple tumour types; validation (e.g., IFN-γ ELISpot using HLA-A\*02:01+ donor PBMCs) is required before any clinical development. KRAS G12C, which enhanced an existing epitope (WB→SB), is the druggable mutation targeted by sotorasib and adagrasib, suggesting a rational combination immunotherapy strategy. The three epitope-destroying mutations (KRAS G13D, KRAS A146T, p53 R249S) represent potential immune-evasion mechanisms. We note that binding prediction alone does not establish immunogenicity: prior work shows that only a small fraction of top-predicted MHC-I binders are confirmed immunogenic, and that peptide–MHC stability predicts immunogenicity better than affinity (Harndahl et al., 2012; Backert and Kohlbacher, 2015), and the binding-to-immunogenicity gap remains the central bottleneck in translating computational epitope prediction to clinical vaccine design.

### Limitations

Training labels are MHCflurry-derived rather than experimental affinities; we quantified the circularity by cross-referencing the 49 IEDB epitopes against MHCflurry's training corpus — 44/49 (89.8%) overlapped, though all five non-overlapping epitopes were correctly classified (5/5, 100%), providing limited evidence of generalisation beyond the training distribution. The single-allele design was deliberate — holding the allele constant isolates label-source variation from allele-driven confounds — but the multi-allele extension addresses the generalisability question directly. The multi-allele estimates rest on IEDB-derived positives; experimental (non-IEDB) validation of the extreme tail is outstanding, and a pMHC-fine-tuned structure model (beyond generic AlphaFold-Multimer) might lift the Type B plateau. The K-E63 salt bridge, all translational predictions, and the cancer neoepitope candidates require experimental confirmation (e.g., IFN-γ ELISpot or tetramer staining). Antigen-processing steps (proteasomal cleavage, TAP transport) are not modelled.

## 5 Conclusion

Architecture choice has a modest effect on peptide-MHC binding prediction: a CNN modestly outperformed a Deep FFN, the top four architectures fell within ~2 percentage points, and a Random Forest was clearly worse. Random-label controls confirmed chance-level performance, ruling out noise overfitting. Canonical binding is largely solved by the anchor motif — a zero-parameter anchor rule matches the trained model — while non-canonical binders plateau at ~0.65 sensitivity across three independent sequence-based predictors, defining the current frontier. Experimental validation of the non-canonical tail is the decisive next step.
