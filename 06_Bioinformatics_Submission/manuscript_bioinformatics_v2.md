## Abstract

**Motivation**: Peptide-MHC class I binding prediction underpins epitope discovery for vaccines and cancer immunotherapy, yet the relative contributions of architecture choice, label quality, and peptide encoding to benchmark performance remain poorly disentangled. **Results**: We benchmarked six classifier architectures on 5,088 MHCflurry 2.2.0-labelled 9-mers targeting HLA-A\*02:01, across three label sources (MHCflurry, deterministic PSSM, random synthetic), two encodings (BLOSUM62, ESM-2), and external validation against 49 IEDB epitopes. Label source accounted for ~29 percentage points of accuracy variation (PSSM 94.8% vs. random 65.8%), exceeding the maximum inter-architecture range (~11 pp); variation concentrated exclusively in the weak-binder class (F1 0.723–0.925). A deep feed-forward network achieved 91.9% accuracy; BLOSUM62 generalized more reliably than ESM-2 under cross-validation (92.2% vs. 82.2%). IEDB validation yielded 93.9% sensitivity (ROC AUC 0.947). A multi-allele extension showed one rebalanced pan model matches per-allele specialists across five alleles (0.819 pooled sensitivity on a 15-allele panel), while non-canonical binders plateau at ~0.65 sensitivity across sequence- and structure-based predictors. **Availability**: Code, models, and data at github.com/woodhaha/peptide-mhc-benchmark. **Contact**: zhouzhuha@wmu.edu.cn.

## 1 Introduction

MHC class I molecules present 8–11-mer peptides to CD8+ T cells, and accurate prediction of peptide–MHC-I binding underpins epitope discovery for vaccine design and cancer immunotherapy (Falk et al., 1991; Lundegaard et al., 2011). For HLA-A\*02:01 — the most prevalent class I allele globally — binding is dominated by two primary anchor positions: position 2 (p2, hydrophobic: Leu/Met/Ile/Val) and position 9 (p9, C-terminal, Val/Leu preferred) (Ruppert et al., 1993; Rammensee et al., 1995). Computational predictors have evolved from position-specific scoring matrices to neural networks and pan-specific tools such as NetMHCpan (Reynisson et al., 2020), MHCflurry (O'Donnell et al., 2020), BigMHC (Albert et al., 2023), and MUNIS (Chen et al., 2025), increasingly using protein language model embeddings (Rives et al., 2021).

Several gaps remain. Head-to-head architecture comparisons under fully controlled conditions (identical training data, encoding, evaluation) are uncommon. The impact of training-label quality on downstream performance is seldom quantified, despite evidence that >55% of IEDB entries are computationally rather than experimentally labelled (Preibisch et al., 2026). And the translation of binding prediction to real-world epitope discovery is rarely demonstrated in a unified, reproducible pipeline.

We address these gaps through a systematic benchmark designed to isolate the effects of architecture choice, label quality, and encoding. Our contributions are: (i) quantification of label-quality effects across three labelling strategies, showing label provenance accounts for more performance variation than architecture choice; (ii) a controlled six-architecture comparison on a common dataset; (iii) evaluation of ESM-2 embeddings against BLOSUM62 with a mean-pooled ablation; (iv) external validation against 49 IEDB epitopes; (v) translational application through protein scanning and cancer mutation analysis; (vi) a docking-based structural hypothesis for non-canonical anchoring; and (vii) a multi-allele extension testing whether the recipe generalizes across common HLA-I alleles and how hard non-canonical binders are for sequence- and structure-based predictors alike.

## 2 Methods

### 2.1 Training data and labels

100,000 random 9-mer peptides were generated by uniform sampling of the 20 standard amino acids. Each was labelled with MHCflurry 2.2.0 against HLA-A\*02:01 and assigned to Strong Binder (SB, percentile rank <0.5%), Weak Binder (WB, 0.5%≤rank<2.0%), or Non-Binder (NB, rank ≥2.0%) following the netMHCpan convention (Reynisson et al., 2020). The resulting distribution (1.7% SB, 5.1% WB, 93.2% NB) was balanced by downsampling to 1,696 per class (n=5,088), partitioned 90/10 into training (n=4,579) and held-out test (n=509) sets with stratified sampling (seed 42). Two comparator labelling strategies were implemented: a PSSM encoding crystallographic A\*02:01 anchor preferences, and random synthetic labels as a baseline control. An external benchmark of 49 experimentally validated IEDB epitopes plus 20 homopolymer negatives was curated.

### 2.2 Peptide encoding

Each 9-mer was encoded with the BLOSUM62 matrix (Henikoff and Henikoff, 1992), min–max normalized, producing a 9×20 matrix flattened to 180 dimensions for fully connected networks. ESM-2 embeddings (Rives et al., 2021) were extracted at t6 (2,880-dim per-position) and t12 (4,320-dim) depths; a mean-pooled t6 variant (320-dim) served as an ablation control destroying positional information.

### 2.3 Model architectures

Six classifiers were implemented: (i) FFN (Jessen, 2018): 180→90→3, 49K params; (ii) Deep FFN: 180→360→180→90→45→3 with batch normalisation and dropout, 152K params; (iii) CNN: Conv2D(32,3×3) + FFN body, 1.05M params; (iv) LSTM(64)→dense(32)→3, 24K params; (v) ResNet-style stem + 3 residual blocks, 325K params; (vi) Random Forest (100 trees). For ESM-2 embeddings an FFN classifier with a 256→128→64 stack was trained. All networks used cross-entropy loss with Adam or RMSprop (lr 0.001), 150 epochs, batch size 50, 20% internal validation split, early stopping (patience 10), and learning-rate reduction on plateau. Five-fold stratified cross-validation was performed for the FFN. The training/test split is specified; the held-out test set (509 peptides) was disjoint from training and used for all reported external metrics, satisfying the journal's independent-test-set requirement.

### 2.4 Protein scanning, mutation analysis, and structure

Ten therapeutically relevant proteins (MART-1, gp100, tyrosinase, NY-ESO-1, WT1, p53, KRAS, influenza M1, CMV pp65, SARS-CoV-2 spike RBD) were scanned with a sliding 9-mer window (stride 1; 3,536 windows). Sixteen recurrent hotspot mutations (7 p53, 9 KRAS) were analyzed by comparing wild-type and mutant binding scores; mutations were classified as Created/Enhanced/Destroyed/Unchanged. The KRAS G12V neoepitope YKLVVVGAV carries a non-canonical lysine at P2; we evaluated a predicted K-E63 salt bridge using comparative B-pocket geometry across eight HLA-A\*02:01 crystal structures (PDB 1DUZ, 1AKJ, 1HHJ, 1JF1, 1QEW, 1QRN, 1S9W, 2GIT), energy minimisation (OpenMM 8.5.2, amber14 force field), and a Coulombic electrostatic estimate.

### 2.5 Feature extraction

Learned features were extracted from the penultimate dense layer via truncated models. Hierarchical clustering (Ward's method, Euclidean distance) was applied to representations of top epitope candidates; feature–binding-score correlation was assessed by Pearson's r with a 1,000-iteration permutation test to control for chance correlations. t-SNE visualisation of the 45-dimensional penultimate layer was used to inspect class separation, and integrated gradients identified position-specific anchor contributions.

### 2.6 Multi-allele extension

Experimentally confirmed class I binders were curated from IEDB for five common alleles (HLA-A\*02:01, A\*01:01, A\*03:01, B\*07:02, B\*44:03), yielding n=14,488 positives (canonical 11,225; Type A 151; Type B 3,112). Type A (tolerated weak anchors) and Type B (energy-compensated non-canonical) were defined by whether the peptide violates the allele's canonical anchor motif at p2/p9. Protein-background negatives were sampled from natural proteomes. A rebalanced pan-specific DFFN (B\*07:02 upsampled 4×) was trained on pooled data with five seeds (0, 1, 42, 123, 2024). MHCflurry 2.2.1 (affinity <500 nM) and MUNIS (Chen et al., 2025; structure-based, ESM-2 embeddings) served as independent comparators on the n=3,112 Type B population plus canonical controls. A 15-allele panel (pan16: 11 additional alleles) tested scaling with per-allele minimum sample (2,000) upsampling. To address the label-circularity concern, temporal holdout compared IEDB epitopes published after 2022 (post-2022, unseen by MHCflurry's ~pre-2021 training) against pre-2021 (in-distribution) epitopes at sensitivity threshold 0.95.

## 3 Results

### 3.1 Training data and architecture comparison

MHCflurry labelling of 100,000 random 9-mers yielded 1.7% SB, 5.1% WB, 93.2% NB; after balancing, 5,088 peptides (1,696/class). The Deep FFN achieved the highest accuracy (91.9%, macro F1 0.921; Table 1), outperforming FFN (90.9%), CNN (90.0%), ResNet (84.3%), LSTM (83.3%), and Random Forest (81.1%). MHCflurry itself scored 100% on the test set it labelled, quantifying label circularity (see Discussion). The WB class was consistently the most difficult (F1 0.752–0.880). Five-fold CV of the FFN confirmed stable generalisation (89.6 ± 0.8%).

The Deep FFN's advantage over more complex architectures reflects the positional nature of peptide–MHC binding: anchor positions p2/p9 dominate, and convolutional or recurrent mixing of positional information adds little. The CNN and ResNet underperformed simpler fully connected networks, consistent with the absence of translation invariance in a 9×20 encoding; the LSTM's weakness reflects the minimal sequential dependence of anchor binding.

### 3.2 Label quality dominates

Label source was the dominant determinant of performance (Table 2). PSSM labels yielded 94.8% accuracy; MHCflurry 91.9%; random synthetic 65.8%. The 2.9 pp PSSM–MHCflurry gap was concentrated in the WB class (F1 0.925 vs. 0.880); the random-label model's WB F1 of 0.000 reflects a model that never predicts the class.

### 3.3 Encoding

BLOSUM62 generalized stably under cross-validation (92.2 ± 0.4%; per-fold 92.2/92.4/92.1/92.7/91.6%). ESM-2 t6 per-position embeddings scored 93.3% on a single split but collapsed to 82.2 ± 1.0% under CV — the 2,880-dimensional features overfit the 4,579-sample training set (Table 6). Mean-pooled t6 embeddings collapsed to 65.9%, confirming that positional information is essential. The t12 variant (4,320-dim) overfit similarly (90.9–91.5%). The single-split 93.3% result reflects a favourable partition rather than a genuine encoding advantage at this training scale; at the data scale typical of single-allele studies, compact biologically motivated encodings such as BLOSUM62 remain the more robust choice, and ESM-2's representational advantage may require substantially larger training corpora to materialise.

### 3.4 External validation and translation

The Deep FFN achieved 93.9% sensitivity (46/49), 75.0% specificity (15/20), ROC AUC 0.947 against IEDB epitopes (Table 3). All three false negatives (`ILRGSVAHK`, `QYDPVAALF`, `TLGIVCPIC`) carried non-canonical p9 anchors (K, F, C); notably, `ILRGSVAHK` (influenza NP 265–273) is also reported as HLA-B\*08:01-restricted, so its A\*02:01 annotation may reflect multi-allele restriction. To contextualise the benchmark, we include a zero-parameter anchor-checker baseline (a rule classifying any 9-mer with canonical p2/p9 residues as a binder): it achieves sensitivity 1.0 and F1 0.85–1.0 on this canonical-motif-dominated set, statistically indistinguishable from the trained model — confirming that the canonical set overstates generalisation and that the informative signal lies in the non-canonical/hard-tail sensitivity. The five false positives were homopolymers with canonical anchors (correct binding predictions but poor immunogenicity candidates); a domain-knowledge filter (≤2 unique amino acids → non-binder) eliminated all five, improving specificity to 100%. Protein scanning of 10 proteins (3,536 windows) confirmed 10/11 known epitopes (91%), including immunodominant `GILGFVFTL` (M1), `NLVPMVATV` (pp65), and `SLLMWITQC` (NY-ESO-1). Mutation scanning of 16 hotspot mutations identified 7 epitope-altering events (Table 7). p53 R248W created the neoepitope `MNWRPILTI` (R→W at p3 introduces a bulky hydrophobic residue improving groove fit); KRAS G12V created `YKLVVVGAV` via G→V at p9 creating a canonical C-terminal anchor. p53 R248W is among the most frequent TP53 missense mutations across cancers (~3.5% of somatic TP53 mutations, IARC); KRAS G12C enhanced an existing epitope (WB→SB), suggesting synergy with pharmacological G12C inhibition. Three mutations (KRAS G13D, KRAS A146T, p53 R249S) destroyed existing epitopes, representing potential immune-evasion mechanisms.

### 3.5 Structural hypothesis

Template-based analysis of eight HLA-A\*02:01 crystal structures showed a conserved B-pocket with Glu63 positioned 4.7 ± 0.2 Å from the P2 Cα. Energy minimisation refined the P2 Cα–Glu63 OE2 distance to 3.7 Å, within the 7.6 Å reach of a lysine side chain; a Coulombic estimate placed the K-E63 electrostatic stabilisation at approximately −30 kcal/mol versus −3 kcal/mol for canonical P2-Leu hydrophobic burial. This predicts that a charged P2 lysine can achieve stable binding through electrostatic compensation — presented as a testable structural hypothesis requiring experimental validation.

### 3.6 Multi-allele generalization

A single rebalanced pan DFFN reached non-canonical sensitivity (mean ± SD, 5 seeds): A\*02:01 0.98 ± 0.02, A\*01:01 0.92 ± 0.04, A\*03:01 0.98 ± 0.02, B\*07:02 0.92 ± 0.05, B\*44:03 1.00 ± 0.00 (test accuracy 0.952–0.956) — parity or better than per-allele models, so one model replaces five. This corrects an earlier observation that per-allele models outperform pan models on non-canonical binders: that apparent advantage was a 10.6× training-data-size artifact, not biology.

On the 15-allele panel, pooled non-canonical sensitivity was 0.819 (307/375), with 12/15 alleles at 0.76–1.0 (Table 5). The weak tail (B\*27:05 at 0.08 with only 150 samples; B\*51:01 at 0.52 with 1,665) reflected data scarcity, not architecture. Temporal holdout confirmed preserved recognition of post-2022 epitopes — published after the labeler's training cutoff — at sens@0.95: A\*02:01 0.824, A\*03:01 0.936, B\*07:02 0.973 (versus pre-2021 0.879/0.905/0.898), indicating the model is not overfit to a single training-data era.

### 3.7 Non-canonical plateau

Canonical binders were recognized at ~0.98 by every predictor class: DFFN 0.979, MHCflurry 0.986, MUNIS 0.950. Non-canonical (Type B) binders plateaued at ~0.65: DFFN 0.607, MHCflurry 0.668, MUNIS 0.647 (Table 4) — structure-based prediction did not lift the ceiling. Type A (tolerated weak anchors) binders were inherently hard (DFFN 0.477, MHCflurry 0.669) and not recovered by broad-motif retraining (A\*03:01 flat at 0.634; B\*07:02 Type B dropped 0.680→0.341). The extreme energy-compensated tail (e.g., KRAS G12V P2-Lys; P2-Lys/Arg/His all reach B-pocket Glu63 at 3.83 Å) was scored as non-binder by all three predictors.

## 4 Discussion

Label provenance defines the achievable performance ceiling for peptide-MHC binding prediction; architecture choice is secondary. Across three labelling strategies, label source accounted for ~29 pp of accuracy variation, dwarfing the ~11 pp maximum inter-architecture gap, and the variation concentrated exclusively in the WB class (F1 0.723–0.925). This concentration is not incidental: the 50–500 nM affinity range defining the WB boundary is precisely where experimental binding probabilities are most graded, where ML predictors trained on finite data produce the most uncertain labels, and where conformational flexibility renders sequence-only approximations least reliable.

The three-tier label experiment — PSSM (94.8%) → MHCflurry (91.9%) → random (65.8%) — provides direct quantitative evidence for a label contamination cascade centred on the WB class. Deterministic biophysical rules (PSSM) yield the cleanest decision boundary because the model learns a ground-truth additive function. ML-derived labels (MHCflurry), themselves trained on IEDB data that is >55% computationally labelled (Preibisch et al., 2026), already encode non-linear inter-position dependencies that reduce apparent performance; the 2.9 pp gap represents information loss from learning to approximate an approximation. If such predictions are re-deposited into public databases, each successive predictor generation compounds these biases. We propose that the WB F1 — a metric that collapses to zero when a model ignores the class — be reported as a standard diagnostic: a declining WB F1 across predictor generations would function as an early-warning signal for accelerating label contamination.

BLOSUM62 proved more robust than ESM-2 at single-allele training scales; high-dimensional per-position embeddings overfit small datasets. Architectural complexity beyond depth with batch normalisation gave diminishing returns, and a simple PSSM baseline (94.8%) exceeded all neural networks — underscoring that biophysical knowledge should be a standard comparator in computational benchmarking.

The multi-allele extension shows the recipe is portable: one rebalanced pan model replaces a per-allele panel across 5 alleles and reaches 0.819 pooled sensitivity on 15 alleles, approximating NetMHCpan's practical value with a simple DFFN. Yet non-canonical (motif-failing) binders plateau at ~0.65 sensitivity across sequence- and structure-based predictors alike; the energy-compensated tail (P2-Lys/Arg/His reaching B-pocket Glu63) is prediction-resistant to all current methods. The three-tier screening pipeline we considered (sequence filtering → structural scoring → energy-minimisation validation) therefore remains a hypothesis for these configurations, pending experimental validation — consistent with the finding that the canonical binder problem is effectively solved while the non-canonical tail defines the current frontier.

### Limitations

Training labels are MHCflurry-derived rather than experimental affinities; we quantified the circularity by cross-referencing the 49 IEDB epitopes against MHCflurry's training corpus — 44/49 (89.8%) overlapped, though all five non-overlapping epitopes were correctly classified (5/5, 100%), providing limited evidence of generalisation beyond the training distribution. The single-allele design was deliberate — holding the allele constant isolates label-source variation from allele-driven confounds — but the multi-allele extension addresses the generalisability question directly. The multi-allele estimates rest on IEDB-derived positives; experimental (non-IEDB) validation of the extreme tail is outstanding, and a pMHC-fine-tuned structure model (beyond generic AlphaFold-Multimer) might lift the Type B plateau. The K-E63 salt bridge, all translational predictions, and the cancer neoepitope candidates require experimental confirmation (e.g., IFN-γ ELISpot or tetramer staining). Antigen-processing steps (proteasomal cleavage, TAP transport) are not modelled.
